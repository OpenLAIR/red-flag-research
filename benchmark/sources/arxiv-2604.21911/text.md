## Appendix E Limitations and Societal Impact

Limitations.
Our approach leverages data primarily generated by the model itself to improve robustness against prompt-induced hallucinations.
While this procedure yields promising results, it introduces a dependency on the LVLM at hand. For instance, if the model fails to incorporate a hint properly, the modified answer may not reflect the intended hint, which subsequently affects quality of the generated preference pair responses.

Another limitation arises from our strategy to keep or discard the generated contrsative responses based on their distance in a sentence embedding space. This distance serves as a proxy for measuring their contrast and identifying samples that provide stronger preference signals. However, embedding distance does not always perfectly capture semantic contrast. Factors such as answer length, phrasing, or content differences may influence the distance without necessarily reflecting meaningful preference differences. Our weighted DPO strategy partially mitigates this issue by assigning different importance to samples based on their estimated informativeness, but the measure remains an imperfect proxy.

Societal Impact. The impact of our work can be seen in similar light to other LVLM hallucination mitigation approaches. Since LVLMs experience a widespread usage among research community and public, detection and mitigation of hallucinations carry a lot of importance. Just as any other technology, human actors with malicious intent can misuse the method for ill-suited purposes. For instance, switching the rejected and chosen answers during DPO can make the fine-tuned model more prone to hallucinations. Additional human checks should safeguard against such scenarios and ensure that the method is applied as intended. Nevertheless, LVLM outputs that are more grounded in visual input should lead to improved safety and trust in the models. Hence, we expect and hope that our research should have a net positive social impact.

### 3.1 Motivation

While existing benchmarks [[34](#bib.bib34), [51](#bib.bib51), [29](#bib.bib29), [38](#bib.bib38), [17](#bib.bib17), [10](#bib.bib10)] assess hallucination, they do not disentangle different contributing factors, such as visual perception errors, the model’s prior knowledge about object co-occurrence, or textual presuppositions.
To address this gap
, we propose HalluScope, a benchmark designed to systematically assess how different causes influence hallucinations.
The benchmark comprises a diverse set of images, each paired with questions to evaluate distinct hallucination factors:

We evaluate the visual pereception of the model through Positive Object Recognition questions, querying the presence of objects visually present in the image, and Random Object Recognition questions, querying the presence of objects that are not present in the image and are unrelated to the scene.

Next, we measure the extent of hallucinations induced when the model relies on learned object co-occurence, instead of visual evidence, through Adversarial Object Recognition questions. These questions query the presence of semantically plausible but visually absent objects. For example, if an image shows a table but no chair, the model may still answer “yes” when asked whether a chair is present, revealing reliance on learned co-occurrence biases rather than the image content.

Lastly, the Adversarial Presupposition Attribute questions introduce an additional level of difficulty by querying an attribute (e.g., color) of an adversarial object whose presence is implicitly assumed in the question. This subset evaluates whether the model relies on the presuppositions in the textual instruction or on grounded visual evidence. For example, a question such as “What is the color of the chair?” implicitly assumes the chair’s presence and can induce the model to hallucinate one.

In the remainder of this section, we describe the construction of this benchmark.

#### C.2.5 Preference Pair Construction.

As described in the main paper, for each sample we construct a preference pair consisting of a chosen and a rejected response.
To generate this contrastive pair, unilateral hint augmentation adds a hint about the presence or absence of objects to the task query. The hint itself may be correct or incorrect: a correct hint encourages a correct response to generate a chosen answer (e.g., confirming the absence of an absent object for CPQ queries), while an incorrect hint encourages an erroneous response to generate a rejected answer (e.g., suggesting the absence of a present object for TPQ and POPE queries). In the case of contrastive hint augmentaion, two contrastive hints are provided to induce different responses.

The Sentence-BERT (SBERT) embeddings of candidate chosen and rejected responses are compared, and if the cosine distance between them exceeds a predefined threshold, the sample is retained (with cosine distance serving as a proxy for detecting meaningfully different responses). We set the distance threshold to 0.90.9 for all task queries, except for the *post-hoc hallucination injection description samples*, for which we adopt a threshold of 0.950.95.

## 2 Related work

Evaluating Hallucinations in LVLMs.
Hallucinations in LVLMs manifest in several forms. In open-ended text generation, hallucination commonly refers to producing content that is not grounded in the visual input. This is typically evaluated using metrics such as CHAIR [[34](#bib.bib34)], and more recently SHR [[51](#bib.bib51)] and ALOHa [[29](#bib.bib29)]. Another form of hallucination arises when the model generates incorrect attributes for objects in the image, as evaluated by benchmarks such as MMHalBench [[38](#bib.bib38)].
In closed-ended settings, where prompts query the presence or properties of specific objects, hallucinations are defined as incorrect responses. This form is commonly assessed using benchmarks such as POPE [[17](#bib.bib17)] and HallusionBench [[10](#bib.bib10)].
While these benchmarks measure outcome-level correctness, they provide limited insight into the relative importance of different causes of hallucinations.
  
Mitigating Hallucinations in LVLMs.
Previous studies to address hallucinations can be grouped into two main categories: post-hoc (training-free) methods and optimization-based methods. Training-free approaches are based on different hypotheses that motivate their strategies: Detecting and intervening on “hallucination attention heads” [[47](#bib.bib47)], re-distributing attention away from semantically uninformative, i.e., sink, tokens [[13](#bib.bib13)], steering hidden representations [[21](#bib.bib21), [28](#bib.bib28), [48](#bib.bib48)]
, leveraging intermediate representations to detect and suppress hallucinations [[40](#bib.bib40), [39](#bib.bib39)], accumulating and reinforcing visually relevant information [[1](#bib.bib1), [5](#bib.bib5)], contrasting the outputs when given the original and noised image [[15](#bib.bib15)] and amplifying attention to visual tokens and contrasting logits between visual and text-only inputs [[22](#bib.bib22)] fall into the first category.
Such methods typically increase inference time [[15](#bib.bib15)] or require modifications to the model’s implementation (e.g., attention calculations [[47](#bib.bib47)]), and are inherently limited to the hypotheses they consider [[13](#bib.bib13)], leaving other sources of hallucination unaddressed.
Optimization-based methods address hallucinations more fundamentally by directly adjusting the LVLM’s weights, allowing the model to systematically align its outputs with grounded visual information and learned preferences.
[[19](#bib.bib19)] proposes a large-scale instruction-tuning dataset including samples with misleading and hallucination-inducing instructions. Another line of work frames hallucination as a preference selection problem and addresses it by optimizing the model on a dataset that provides relevant preference signals [[51](#bib.bib51), [52](#bib.bib52), [25](#bib.bib25), [45](#bib.bib45), [49](#bib.bib49), [9](#bib.bib9), [43](#bib.bib43)]. Datasets used in these works rely on closed-source vision models to analyze images and detect hallucinations [[49](#bib.bib49)], or they combine closed-source models with richly annotated datasets such as Visual Genome [[51](#bib.bib51)], which limits reproducibility and scalability. Furthermore, these datasets typically focus on hallucinations in general, rather than those specifically driven by textual priors.

### 4.2 Preference Dataset Construction

To train HalluVL-DPO, we build a large-scale preference dataset comprising 27.4k images and over 100k queries per model for both LLaVA-1.5-7B and Qwen2-VL-7B. Our pipeline, inspired by [[43](#bib.bib43)], uses [[7](#bib.bib7)] for image generation and Grounding-DINO [[23](#bib.bib23)] for visual verification. Each dataset sample consists of an image, a textual instruction, and a pair of responses: a preferred, visually-grounded chosen answer (ywy\_{w}) and a hallucinated, rejected answer (yly\_{l}).
We focus on three primary task types:

Presupposition Questions: Queries asking about an object’s attributes while implicitly assuming its presence. These are categorized as True Presupposition Questions (TPQ) when the object exists in the image and Counterfactual Presupposition Questions (CPQ) when it is absent. They are are generated via GPT5-mini.

Object Existence: POPE-style questions directly querying the presence or absence of objects.

Detailed Descriptions: Open-ended queries requiring comprehensive scene analysis.

The responses are generated such that the chosen ones are more visually grounded (i.e., less hallucinated) than the rejected ones. To induce or prevent hallucinations, the model is prompted with either a correct hint or an incorrect hint , indicating the presence or absence of one or more objects, appended to the original instruction. We refer to this as the unilateral hint augmentation strategy. If the resulting response is far from the original output in a sentence embedding space (determined via a predefined threshold), the sample is included in the preference dataset. Notably, this strategy keeps the distribution of training dataset responses close to the model being optimized, which is crucial for an efficient DPO [[49](#bib.bib49)]. See [Section C.2](#A3.SS2 "C.2 Preference Dataset Construction ‣ Appendix C HalluVL-DPO Framework ‣ When Prompts Override Vision: Prompt-Induced Hallucinations in LVLMs") for further details on the pipeline. Example samples from the dataset are illustrated in [Figure 4](#S4.F4 "In 4.2 Preference Dataset Construction ‣ 4 HalluVL-DPO Framework ‣ When Prompts Override Vision: Prompt-Induced Hallucinations in LVLMs").
  
A limitation with standard unilateral hint augmentation strategy is that it can introduce reward hacking through length bias [[37](#bib.bib37), [4](#bib.bib4)] for description queries, i.e. biasing the model to generate very concise or verbose descriptions (examples in [Appendix D](#A4 "Appendix D Additional Experimental Analysis ‣ When Prompts Override Vision: Prompt-Induced Hallucinations in LVLMs")).
We propose two strategies, Contrastive Hint Augmentation and Post-Hoc Hallucination Injection to address this issue.
Additionally, to improve the diversity of hallucinated content in the generated preference pairs we introduce Model-Assisted Answer Inversion.

Contrastive Hint Augmentation: Unlike unilateral prompting, correct or incorrect hints are added to both the chosen and rejected instructions. This ensures length consistency between responses while maintaining a sharp preference signal.

Post-Hoc Hallucination Injection: We first generate a multi-sentence description from the target LVLM. We then select one sentence to modify, sampled with a probability that increases linearly with the sentence index (i.e., later sentences are more likely to be chosen). The selected sentence is replaced with a hallucination-inducing prefix asserting the presence of an absent object, and the model is prompted to complete the description. This preserves the original fluency and structure while integrating hallucinated content.

Model-Assisted Answer Inversion: Since the hints used for augmentation are coarse-grained (e.g., indicating the presence or absence of an object), they provide limited control over the attributes mentioned in the generated responses, resulting in low diversity in the resulting preference pairs. To enrich the diversity beyond what can be obtained with these hints, we employ an external LLM to generate additional hallucinated responses conditioned on the original question and the correct answer. This strategy is applied specifically to TPQs. The LLM is prompted to produce a rejected response that preserves the presence of the target object while assigning it incorrect attributes related to the question. Such responses introduce more challenging negative examples that target fine-grained visual reasoning. We refer to these samples as Attribute queries.

It is worth noting that the above propositions preserve the advantage in keeping the distribution of generated responses close to the target model [[49](#bib.bib49)].

### 4.1 Weighted Preference Optimization

To mitigate prompt-induced hallucinations, we propose a variant of Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) [[32](#bib.bib32)] based on sample-informativeness.
Our framework finetunes the LVLM by optimizing an offline dataset of chosen (ywy\_{w}) and rejected (yly\_{l}) response pairs to increase the likelihood of preferred, visually-grounded outputs.
Standard DPO typically treats all pair samples with equal priority; however, we argue that not all preference pairs are equally informative for the model’s alignment and we introduce a sample-informativeness weight w⁡(x,yw,yl)w(x,y\_{w},y\_{l}) that reflects the estimated informativeness of the preference pair.

We define our weight w⁡(x,yw,yl)w(x,y\_{w},y\_{l}) based on the semantic gap between the chosen and rejected responses:

Low Informativeness (w⁡(x,yw,yl)=1w(x,y\_{w},y\_{l})=1): Preference pairs that are nearly identical or simple rephrasings provide a weak learning signal and receive lower weights.

Moderate Informativeness (w⁡(x,yw,yl)=2w(x,y\_{w},y\_{l})=2): Pairs where both responses may follow an incorrect presupposition but differ in the level of detail or relevance.

High Informativeness (w⁡(x,yw,yl)=3w(x,y\_{w},y\_{l})=3): Pairs with a clear contrast, such as when the chosen answer correctly identifies the absence of an object while the rejected answer assumes its presence.

[Figure 3](#S4.F3 "In 4.1 Weighted Preference Optimization ‣ 4 HalluVL-DPO Framework ‣ When Prompts Override Vision: Prompt-Induced Hallucinations in LVLMs") illustrates these situations.
To estimate this semantic gap, we prompt an external LLM (prompt provided in [Section C.1](#A3.SS1 "C.1 Weighted Preference Optimization ‣ Appendix C HalluVL-DPO Framework ‣ When Prompts Override Vision: Prompt-Induced Hallucinations in LVLMs")) to assess the contrast between the response pairs. This process acts as an additional layer of verification, down-weighting noisy or ambiguous pairs and focusing the optimization on samples that provide a clear and reliable grounding signal.

### 3.2 Benchmark Construction

![Refer to caption](2604.21911v1/Figures/Mitigating_Hallucinations/aohd_construction_improved_8.png)
![Refer to caption](2604.21911v1/Figures/Mitigating_Hallucinations/example_halluscope_3.png)

A. Diverse Sample Pool. We construct a semantically diverse subset of COCO images that covers a wide range of semantic content. To this end, we embed all captions using a sentence transformer and apply a KK-Center Greedy algorithm to the resulting embeddings, iteratively selecting samples that are maximally distant from one another in embedding space. Because captions reflect the semantic content of images, this procedure yields a semantically diverse set of images.
  
B. Object Detection.
We employ a robust pipeline to identify all objects present in each image within our selected sample pool. Candidate objects are sourced from (1) COCO instances annotations, which provide high-quality, human-verified object labels, and (2) detections produced by an object detection model (Florence-2-large [[44](#bib.bib44)]), which expands coverage to a more diverse and fine-grained set of objects. To check for the presence or absence of a candidate object in an image, we employ a two-stage verification pipeline. (1)
An object grounding model (Grounding-DINO [[23](#bib.bib23)]) is first prompted with the name of each candidate object to obtain a grounding confidence score. When no score is returned, indicating that no corresponding region is detected, the object is labeled absent. When a score is returned, it is compared to a predefined threshold: scores exceeding the threshold indicate presence, whereas for lower-confidence cases
, (2) an LVLM [[41](#bib.bib41)] is subsequently queried about the object’s presence. In the left example of [Figure 2](#S3.F2 "In 3.2 Benchmark Construction ‣ 3 HalluScope: Diagnosing Hallucinations in LVLMs ‣ When Prompts Override Vision: Prompt-Induced Hallucinations in LVLMs"), the object detector initially identifies a baseball cap, sunglasses, chair, belt, baseball bat, and person; the verification stage subsequently removes the baseball cap from the set of present objects.
  
C. Object Co-occurrence and Context-aware Adversarial Object Discovery. Our goal is to annotate each image with three objects: a present 𝒪present\mathcal{O}\_{\text{present}}, a random object 𝒪random\mathcal{O}\_{\text{random}}, and an adversarial object 𝒪adversarial\mathcal{O}\_{\text{adversarial}}.
To identify adversarial objects, that are contextually credible yet visually absent, we analyze object co-occurrence patterns. LVLMs are typically trained on large-scale datasets where objects frequently appear together in consistent semantic contexts, leading models to predict the presence of one object when another appears, even in the absence of supporting visual evidence [[27](#bib.bib27), [30](#bib.bib30)]. Consequently, these contextually credible but absent objects are a key source of hallucination and a natural way to probe over-reliance on the model’s learned knowledge.
  
Leveraging the verified labels from the previous step, we construct an object co-occurrence graph
and compute pairwise pointwise mutual information (PMI) [[6](#bib.bib6)] scores between objects (nodes) as edge scores. For each image, to discover the most credible adversarial object, each node is assigned a cumulative association score defined as the sum of its edge weights with all objects (nodes) present in the image. Nodes are then ranked according to this score, and the highest-ranked ones are selected as adversarial candidates. The absence of these candidates is subsequently evaluated using the aforementioned two-stage verification pipeline.
The first candidate verified to be visually absent is selected as 𝒪adversarial\mathcal{O}\_{\text{adversarial}}. Among the objects present in the image, we then select the object with the highest PMI edge score relative to the adversarial object as the corresponding 𝒪present\mathcal{O}\_{\text{present}}. 𝒪random\mathcal{O}\_{\text{random}} is sampled uniformly from objects that do not appear in the image.
[Figure 2](#S3.F2 "In 3.2 Benchmark Construction ‣ 3 HalluScope: Diagnosing Hallucinations in LVLMs ‣ When Prompts Override Vision: Prompt-Induced Hallucinations in LVLMs") shows the baseball game scene (left) with verified present objects: a belt, sunglasses, baseball bat, person, and chair. Our PMI-based procedure ranks the top-10 adversarial candidates as baseball player, baseball cap, mound (baseball), baseball base, baseball, baseball glove, knee pad, sports uniform, wristlet, and power outlet; among these, baseball cap is the highest-scoring object absent from the scene and is therefore selected as the adversarial object. The present object paired with it is baseball bat, which has the highest PMI with the adversarial object, while statue is chosen as a random object unrelated to the scene.
  
D. Question Generation.
For each sample, we generate four questions: three object recognition questions querying the presence of 𝒪present\mathcal{O}\_{\text{present}}, 𝒪random\mathcal{O}\_{\text{random}}, and 𝒪adversarial\mathcal{O}\_{\text{adversarial}}, and one adversarial presupposition attribute question that concerns the adversarial object and implicitly assumes its presence. Recognition questions are instantiated by randomly sampling from a set of templates (e.g., “Is a {object} present in the image?”), whereas the adversarial presupposition attribute question is generated via prompting GPT5-mini, conditioned on the image caption and the corresponding 𝒪present\mathcal{O}\_{\text{present}} and 𝒪adversarial\mathcal{O}\_{\text{adversarial}}.
  
For example, in the baseball scene shown in [Figure 2](#S3.F2 "In 3.2 Benchmark Construction ‣ 3 HalluScope: Diagnosing Hallucinations in LVLMs ‣ When Prompts Override Vision: Prompt-Induced Hallucinations in LVLMs"), where 𝒪present\mathcal{O}\_{\text{present}} is baseball bat, 𝒪random\mathcal{O}\_{\text{random}} is statue, and 𝒪adversary\mathcal{O}\_{\text{adversary}} is baseball cap, the resulting questions are: “Does the image contain a baseball bat?”, “Is a statue present in the image?”, “Does the image contain a baseball cap?”, and “What logo appears on the baseball cap worn by the batter?”
  
HalluScope includes 3K images in each subset, where subsets are defined by the source of candidate objects (COCO Instances or Florence object detector).
Our object recognition subset can be viewed as an extension of POPE [[17](#bib.bib17)] but we include over an order of magnitude more images and by not restricting queried objects to a closed set of object categories.
  
It should be noted that our pipeline is highly flexible: images can be drawn from any dataset, candidate objects can come from various sources, and verification can be performed using an ensemble of LVLMs to improve annotation quality even further.

###### Abstract

Despite impressive progress in capabilities of large vision-language models (LVLMs), these systems remain vulnerable to hallucinations, i.e., outputs that are not grounded in the visual input. Prior work has attributed hallucinations in LVLMs to factors such as limitations of the vision backbone or the dominance of the language component, yet the relative importance of these factors remains unclear. To resolve this ambiguity, We propose HalluScope, a benchmark to better understand the extent to which different factors induce hallucinations.
Our analysis indicates that hallucinations largely stem from excessive reliance on textual priors and background knowledge, especially information introduced through textual instructions.
To mitigate hallucinations induced by textual instruction priors, we propose HalluVL-DPO, a framework for fine-tuning off-the-shelf LVLMs towards more visually grounded responses. HalluVL-DPO leverages preference optimization using a curated training dataset that we construct, guiding the model to prefer grounded responses over hallucinated ones.
We demonstrate that our optimized model effectively mitigates the targeted hallucination failure mode, while preserving or improving performance on other hallucination benchmarks and visual capability evaluations.

## 1 Introduction

Large Vision–Language Models (LVLMs) have recently achieved remarkable progress across multimodal tasks such as image captioning, visual reasoning, and instruction following [[20](#bib.bib20), [41](#bib.bib41), [14](#bib.bib14), [42](#bib.bib42), [46](#bib.bib46)]. Despite these advances, they are still prone to visual hallucinations [[38](#bib.bib38), [36](#bib.bib36), [2](#bib.bib2), [12](#bib.bib12)], i.e., generating outputs that are not grounded in the input visual information. Such failures limit the deployment of LVLMs in safety-critical applications such as autonomous systems and medical diagnosis.
While even standalone
visual encoders can exhibit object hallucinations [[24](#bib.bib24), [16](#bib.bib16)],
emerging evidence suggests a different failure mode: even when visual recognition is accurate, modern LVLMs frequently generate incorrect responses, reflecting the dominance of the text modality over the visual modality [[45](#bib.bib45), [15](#bib.bib15), [3](#bib.bib3)]. In such cases, the model’s output is more influenced by the learned object co-occurence priors or the text instruction rather than by the image itself, highlighting the stronger role of language priors in shaping response.

These observations point to a shift in failure modes. As visual backbones improve, hallucinations increasingly arise from conflicts between language priors and visual information, rather than from perceptual limitations alone. However, existing evaluation benchmarks including POPE [[17](#bib.bib17)], CHAIR [[34](#bib.bib34)], SHR [[51](#bib.bib51)], and MMHAL-Bench [[38](#bib.bib38)] do not distinguish between hallucinations originating from perception failures, learned object co-occurrence priors, or presuppositions introduced by the instruction itself. As a result, the relative contribution of these factors remains poorly understood, limiting both diagnosis and mitigation.

![Refer to caption](2604.21911v1/Figures/Mitigating_Hallucinations/main_7.png)

To address this limitation, we introduce HalluScope, a diagnostic benchmark to
isolate distinct
causes of hallucination in LVLMs. Rather than treating hallucination as a single phenomenon, HalluScope separates errors arising from (i) visual perception limitations, (ii) reliance on learned semantic priors, and (iii) reliance on presuppositions introduced by the instruction. Using this benchmark, we demonstrate that hallucinations in modern LVLMs are predominantly driven by textual instructions, even when visual perception remains reliable ([Figure 1](#S1.F1 "In 1 Introduction ‣ When Prompts Override Vision: Prompt-Induced Hallucinations in LVLMs")). These findings suggest that improving LVLM reliability is increasingly an alignment problem between modalities.

To mitigate hallucinations induced by text instruction presuppositions, we propose HalluVL-DPO, a framework for fine-tuning off-the-shelf LVLMs. HalluVL-DPO employs a sample-informativeness based variant of preference-optimization [[32](#bib.bib32)], and leverages a large-scale preference dataset specifically constructed to favor grounded over hallucinated responses. Models fine-tuned by our framework effectively reduce the targeted hallucinations, achieve consistent improvements on hallucination benchmarks, and maintain strong general multimodal performance.
Our contributions are summarized as follows:

A benchmark for assessing the impact of distinct causes of LVLM hallucinations. We introduce HalluScope, a benchmark designed to disentangle distinct causes of hallucination: perception failures, learned object co-occurrence priors, or presuppositions introduced by the instruction. Using HalluScope, we show that hallucinations in modern LVLMs predominantly arise from over-reliance on textual instruction presuppositions and learned semantic priors rather than limitations of visual perception, revealing a shift in failure modes as visual backbones improve.

A framework to effectively mitigate hallucinations caused by over-reliance on textual instruction presuppositions. We propose HalluVL-DPO, an LVLM fine-tuning framework based on sample-informativeness based preference optimization [[32](#bib.bib32)]. Our framework explicitly encourages visually grounded over hallucinated answers, using a challenging, generated training dataset. Our approach effectively reduces targeted hallucinations and show consistent gains on hallucination benchmarks.

We will release our HalluScope benchmark and its construction pipeline, along with all components of HalluVL-DPO, including the training data, dataset construction pipeline, and fine-tuned model checkpoints.

## 4 HalluVL-DPO Framework

In this section, we introduce HalluVL-DPO, a framework for mitigating hallucination failures in LVLMs, particularly those caused by over-reliance on textual instruction presuppositions. Our approach fine-tunes LVLMs using a sample-informativeness based variant of Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) [[32](#bib.bib32)], post-training the model to prefer visually grounded responses over hallucinated ones ([Section 4.1](#S4.SS1 "4.1 Weighted Preference Optimization ‣ 4 HalluVL-DPO Framework ‣ When Prompts Override Vision: Prompt-Induced Hallucinations in LVLMs")).
To enable this fine-tuning, we construct a training dataset where each sample is paired with a preferred response and a rejected, hallucinated one. These response pairs provide explicit supervision for learning to favor more grounded outputs. We discuss the construction of this dataset and explore different strategies for constructing these response pairs in ([Section 4.2](#S4.SS2 "4.2 Preference Dataset Construction ‣ 4 HalluVL-DPO Framework ‣ When Prompts Override Vision: Prompt-Induced Hallucinations in LVLMs")), and further provide a detailed comparison in the ablation experiments ([Section 5.3](#S5.SS3 "5.3 Further Analysis ‣ 5 Experiments ‣ When Prompts Override Vision: Prompt-Induced Hallucinations in LVLMs")).

##### HallusionBench [[10](#bib.bib10)].

HallusionBench is designed to evaluate hallucination robustness in multimodal reasoning tasks.
It contains challenging yes/no questions in which visual or textual cues are intentionally ambiguous, conflicting, or incomplete. The benchmark tests whether models provide correct and consistent yes/no answers.
We follow the official metric definitions and report a score obtained by summing three accuracies:
(1) Atomic Accuracy (aAcc), which measures instance-level correctness as the proportion of correctly answered question–image pairs;
(2) Question Accuracy (qAcc), where a question is counted as correct only if all its associated instances are answered correctly; and
(3) Figure Accuracy (fAcc), where a figure is considered correct only if all questions associated with that image are answered correctly.
In our evaluation, a prediction is marked as correct if the model’s output explicitly contains the ground-truth answer, and the above metrics are computed accordingly.

## 6 Conclusion

In this work, we introduced HalluScope, a diagnostic benchmark designed to disentangle the multifaceted causes of hallucinations in Large Vision-Language Models (LVLMs). Our systematic analysis indicated that hallucinations are primarily driven by an over-reliance on textual instruction, learned object co-occurence priors, and not from perceptual limitations of the visual backbone.
To address this, we proposed HalluVL-DPO, a fine-tuning framework that leverages a sample-informativeness based preference optimization loss. By training on a large-scale, high-quality synthetic preference dataset, our method effectively mitigates targeted prompt-induced hallucinations while preserving general multimodal capabilities.

While our current strategy based on leveraging synthetic images is effective in mitigating hallucinations, future work will explore the integration of real-world and synthetic image mixtures to further enhance robustness across diverse, open-ended scenarios. To foster continued progress in the community, we will publicly release the HalluScope benchmark, our construction pipeline, and the HalluVL-DPO training resources.

We investigate whether data generated by one model can be effectively used to fine-tune another. To this end, we perform a cross-model training experiment, where LLaVA is fine-tuned using the dataset generated by Qwen and, conversely, Qwen is fine-tuned using a dataset generated by LLaVA.

We observe that LLaVA-generated data is less effective for improving Qwen’s AdP score, while largely preserving its recognition performance. In our setting, preference pairs generated by a given model appear to be particularly well suited for fine-tuning that same architecture. Nevertheless, the results also indicate that cross-model data remains beneficial: despite the distribution mismatch, it still leads to meaningful improvements over the base models, demonstrating the overall utility of our generated dataset.

To validate the detected objects, we first use Grounding DINO [[23](#bib.bib23)], which given the name of an object as input, returns candidate bounding boxes that match the text query (object name) with confidence scores when a matching object is detected. For Grounding DINO input, we set the text–region matching threshold and the box (objectness) threshold to 0.40.4 and 0.350.35 respectively, to avoid missing present objects.
However, since the detection confidence returned by Grounding DINO is sensitive to object size, occlusion, and visual prominence, selecting a single confidence threshold that reliably distinguishes object presence from absence across all images remains challenging. In our experiments, we therefore fix this threshold empirically to 0.6 and introduce an additional validation step in a two-stage verification pipeline to address this limitation. In the second stage, a vision–language model (Qwen2-VL-7B) is queried about the presence of the candidate object (*“Is ¡object¿ present in the image?”*) conditioned on both (i) the cropped region proposed by Grounding DINO and (ii) a textual description of the full image generated by the same model. The full image caption provides contextual information that supports more reliable verification. ## 5.1 Implementation setup

**Models and training hyperparameters.** We optimize two LVLMs, LLaVA-1.5-7B and Qwen2-VL-7B, using DPO, starting from the instruct-tuned versions. LoRA [11] is applied to the LLM component of each model, with the DPO $\beta$ parameter set to 0.1.

**Dataset.** We use a total of 20k samples, composed of CPQ, TPQ, POPE, description, and attribute subsets, following a 5:5:6:9:5 composition. The description samples are equally drawn from unilateral and contrastive hint augmentation as well as post-hoc hallucination injection.

**Evaluation benchmarks.** We evaluate our models across diverse multimodal benchmarks. We report results on our proposed HalluScope benchmark separately, and additionally evaluate on a broad collection of existing benchmarks, including POPE, CP-Bench [43], HallusionBench [10], CHAIR [34], MME [8], ScienceQA [26], and MM-Vet [50](evaluated with GPT5-mini).

**Evaluated baselines.** We evaluate the effectiveness of training on our balanced synthetic dataset by comparing the following baselines: (i) the original, non-optimized model; (ii) VCD [15]; (iii) a DPO-fine-tuned model trained using the data composition reported in Antidote [43] (evaluated on LLaVA-1.5-7B) using their hyperparameters, and (iv) with best hyperparameters from our grid search; (v) *HalluVL-DPO*, which applies DPO training on our balanced synthetic dataset; and (vi) its weighted variant, (v), *HalluVL-DPO*$^w$, where samples are reweighted during training based on their estimated difficulty. Greedy decoding is used for all experiments, except for VCD, which employs direct sampling as specified in its original implementation.

Additional training hyperparameters, evaluation benchmarks details, and baseline implementations are provided in Section B.

Adversarial Performance (AdP) is then computed as the percentage of samples for which the adversary object is correctly determined to be absent:

\[
\mathrm{AdP} = \frac {1}{N} \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {N} \mathbb {I} \left[ \text { present } _ {i} = \text { false } \right],
\]

## Abstract

Despite impressive progress in capabilities of large vision-language models (LVLMs), these systems remain vulnerable to hallucinations, i.e., outputs that are not grounded in the visual input. Prior work has attributed hallucinations in LVLMs to factors such as limitations of the vision backbone or the dominance of the language component, yet the relative importance of these factors remains unclear. To resolve this ambiguity, We propose HalluScope, a benchmark to better understand the extent to which different factors induce hallucinations. Our analysis indicates that hallucinations largely stem from excessive reliance on textual priors and background knowledge, especially information introduced through textual instructions. To mitigate hallucinations induced by textual instruction priors, we propose HalluVL-DPO, a framework for fine-tuning off-the-shelf LVLMs towards more visually grounded responses. HalluVL-DPO leverages preference optimization using a curated training dataset that we construct, guiding the model to prefer grounded responses over hallucinated ones. We demonstrate that our optimized model effectively mitigates the targeted hallucination failure mode, while preserving or improving performance on other hallucination benchmarks and visual capability evaluations. To support reproducibility and further research, we will publicly release our evaluation benchmark, preference training dataset, and code \( ^{1} \) .

Large Vision–Language Models (LVLMs) have recently achieved remarkable progress across multimodal tasks such as image captioning, visual reasoning, and instruction following  \( [20, 41, 14, 42, 46] \) . ## 2 Related work

Evaluating Hallucinations in LVLMs. Hallucinations in LVLMs manifest in several forms. In open-ended text generation, hallucination commonly refers to producing content that is not grounded in the visual input. This is typically evaluated using metrics such as CHAIR [34], and more recently SHR [51] and ALOHa [29]. Another form of hallucination arises when the model generates incorrect attributes for objects in the image, as evaluated by benchmarks such as MMHal-Bench [38]. In closed-ended settings, where prompts query the presence or properties of specific objects, hallucinations are defined as incorrect responses. This form is commonly assessed using benchmarks such as POPE [17] and HallusionBench [10]. While these benchmarks measure outcome-level correctness, they provide limited insight into the relative importance of different causes of hallucinations.

Mitigating Hallucinations in LVLMs. Previous studies to address hallucinations can be grouped into two main categories: post-hoc (training-free) methods and optimization-based methods. Training-free approaches are based on different hypotheses that motivate their strategies: Detecting and intervening on “hallucination attention heads” [47], re-distributing attention away from semantically uninformative, i.e., sink, tokens [13], steering hidden representations [21, 28, 48], leveraging intermediate representations to detect and suppress hallucinations [40, 39], accumulating and reinforcing visually relevant information [1, 5], contrasting the outputs when given the original and noised image [15] and amplifying attention to visual tokens and contrasting logits between visual and text-only inputs [22] fall into the first category. Such methods typically increase inference time [15] or require modifications to the model’s implementation (e.g., attention calculations [47]), and are inherently limited to the hypotheses they consider [13], leaving other sources of hallucination unaddressed. Optimization-based methods address hallucinations more fundamentally by directly adjusting the LVLM’s weights, allowing the model to systematically align its outputs with grounded visual information and learned preferences. [19] proposes a large-scale instruction-tuning dataset including samples with misleading and hallucination-inducing instructions. Another line of work frames hallucination as a preference selection problem and addresses it by optimizing the model on a dataset that provides relevant preference signals [51, 52, 25, 45, 49, 9, 43]. Datasets used in these works rely on closed-source vision models to analyze images and detect hallucinations [49], or they combine closed-source models with richly annotated datasets such as Visual Genome [51], which limits reproducibility and scalability. Furthermore, these datasets typically focus on hallucinations in general, rather than those specifically driven by textual priors.

For example, in the baseball scene shown in Figure 2, where  \( O_{present} \)  is baseball bat,  \( O_{random} \)  is statue, and  \( O_{adversary} \)  is baseball cap, the resulting questions are: “Does the image contain a baseball bat?”, “Is a statue present in the image?”, “Does the image contain a baseball cap?”, and “What logo appears on the baseball cap worn by the batter?”

HalluScope includes 3K images in each subset, where subsets are defined by the source of candidate objects (COCO Instances or Florence object detector). Our object recognition subset can be viewed as an extension of POPE [17] but we include over an order of magnitude more images and by not restricting queried objects to a closed set of object categories.

It should be noted that our pipeline is highly flexible: images can be drawn from any dataset, candidate objects can come from various sources, and verification can be performed using an ensemble of LVLMs to improve annotation quality even further.

### Object Co-occurrence and Context-aware Adversary Object Discovery.

Let $\mathcal{I}$ denote the set of all images in the dataset. For any pair of objects $o_a, o_b$, selected from the pool of all detected objects, we define the pointwise mutual information (PMI) between $o_a$ and $o_b$ as:

$$\mathrm{PMI}(o_a, o_b) = \begin{cases} \log \frac{P(o_a, o_b)}{P(o_a) P(o_b)}, & \text{if } P(o_a, o_b) > 0, \\ 0, & \text{otherwise}, \end{cases}$$

where $P(o_a, o_b)$ is the joint probability that objects $o_a$ and $o_b$ co-occur in the same image, and $P(o_a)$ is the marginal probability that object $o_a$ appears in an image, defined as:

$$P(o_a, o_b) = \frac{|\{I : o_a \in I \land o_b \in I\}|}{|\mathcal{I}|}, \qquad P(o_a) = \frac{|\{I : o_a \in I\}|}{|\mathcal{I}|}.$$

Given any image $I$, let $\mathcal{O}_{\text{scene}}^I$ denote the set of objects present in the image. For each candidate object $o_c$, we compute a relatedness score by summing its pointwise mutual information (PMI) with the objects in the scene:

$$\text{score}_I(o_c) = \sum_{o_s \in \mathcal{O}_{\text{scene}}^I} \max \left( \text{PMI}(o_c, o_s), 0 \right).$$

The top-$k$ objects with the highest scores are considered candidate adversarial objects. To ensure the candidate objects are absent from the image, they are subsequently verified using the two-stage object presence verification pipeline.

### 4.2 Preference Dataset Construction

To train HalluVL-DPO, we build a large-scale preference dataset comprising 27.4k images and over 100k queries per model for both LLaVA-1.5-7B and Qwen2-VL-7B. We focus on three primary task types:

- Presupposition Questions: Queries asking about an object's attributes while implicitly assuming its presence. These are categorized as True Presupposition Questions (TPQ) when the object exists in the image and Counterfactual Presupposition Questions (CPQ) when it is absent. They are generated via GPT5-mini.
- Object Existence: POPE-style questions directly querying the presence or absence of objects.
- Detailed Descriptions: Open-ended queries requiring comprehensive scene analysis.

The responses are generated such that the chosen ones are more visually grounded (i.e., less hallucinated) than the rejected ones. To induce or prevent hallucinations, the model is prompted with either a correct hint or an incorrect hint, indicating the presence or absence of one or more objects, appended to the original instruction. We refer to this as the unilateral hint augmentation strategy. ## A.1 Benchmark Construction

### Diverse Sample Pool.

Let $\mathcal{D} = \{x_1, \ldots, x_N\}$ denote the set of all captions, and $f$ be a fixed sentence-embedding function (all-MiniLM-L6-v2 Sentence-BERT model [33] for us). To select a diverse subset $\mathcal{S} \subset \mathcal{D}$ of size $K$, we apply the K-Center Greedy algorithm. It initializes $\mathcal{S}$ with a random sample and then iteratively adds the point maximally distant from the current set in embedding space:

$$x^* = \arg \max_{x \in \mathcal{D} \setminus \mathcal{S}} \min_{s \in \mathcal{S}} \|f(x) - f(s)\|_2.$$

$$\mathcal{S} \leftarrow \mathcal{S} \cup \{x^*\}.$$

This process repeats until $|\mathcal{S} = \{x'_1, \ldots, x'_K\}| = K$, yielding a diverse subset of captions that are maximally spread out in the embedding space.

### Object Detection.

We use Florence2-large [44] as our object detection model. To validate the detected objects, we first use Grounding DINO [23], which given the name of an object as input, returns candidate bounding boxes that match the text query (object name) with confidence scores when a matching object is detected. For Grounding DINO input, we set the text–region matching threshold and the box (objectness) threshold to 0.4 and 0.35 respectively, to avoid missing present objects. However, since the detection confidence returned by Grounding DINO is sensitive to object size, occlusion, and visual prominence, selecting a single confidence threshold that reliably distinguishes object presence from absence across all images remains challenging. In our experiments, we therefore fix this threshold empirically to 0.6 and introduce an additional validation step in a two-stage verification pipeline to address this limitation. In the second stage, a vision–language model (Qwen2-VL-7B) is queried about the presence of the candidate object (“Is object$_{i}$ present in the image?”) conditioned on both (i) the cropped region proposed by Grounding DINO and (ii) a textual description of the full image generated by the same model. Prior work has shown that reasoning over localized regions improves object existence verification compared to using the full image alone [5]. The full image caption provides contextual information that supports more reliable verification. ### C.2 Preference Dataset Construction

Our dataset generation pipeline is inspired by [43]. Below, we provide a more detailed description of the dataset creation process.

### C.2.1 Caption Pool Construction.

In this first stage, raw CC3M [35] captions are filtered for fluency, syntactic validity, and concreteness. Syntactic structure is verified using spaCy's en_core_web_trf transformer model. Each retained caption is verified to satisfy the above conditions. We acquire 30k filtered captions from 413.7k raw captions.

We prompt GPT5-mini to rewrite each filtered caption into a Stable Diffusion prompt and generate a structured JSON listing the present in the caption and candidate adversary objects.

### C.2.3 Image Generation and Grounded Object Verification

We generate images using Stable Diffusion 3(medium) [7] by providing each sample's rewritten caption and a negative prompt. ## 4 HalluVL-DPO Framework

In this section, we introduce HalluVL-DPO, a framework for mitigating hallucination failures in LVLMs, particularly those caused by over-reliance on textual instruction presuppositions. Our approach fine-tunes LVLMs using a sample-informativeness based variant of Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) [32], post-training the model to prefer visually grounded responses over hallucinated ones (Section 4.1). To enable this fine-tuning, we construct a training dataset where each sample is paired with a preferred response and a rejected, hallucinated one. These response pairs provide explicit supervision for learning to favor more grounded outputs. We discuss the construction of this dataset and explore different strategies for constructing these response pairs in (Section 4.2), and further provide a detailed comparison in the ablation experiments (Section 5.3).

### 4.1 Weighted Preference Optimization

To mitigate prompt-induced hallucinations, we propose a variant of Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) [32] based on sample-informativeness. Our framework finetunes the LVLM by optimizing an offline dataset of chosen (yw) and rejected (yl) response pairs to increase the likelihood of preferred, visually-grounded outputs. Standard DPO typically treats all pair samples with equal priority; however, we argue that not all preference pairs are equally informative for the model's alignment and we introduce a sample-informativeness weight w(x, yw, yl) that reflects the estimated informativeness of the preference pair. Using rw(x) = log (πθ(yw|x)/πref(yw|x)) and rl(x) = log (πθ(yl|x)/πref(yl|x)) the log-probability ratios relative to the reference model πref, our weighted loss is defined as:

$$\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{DPO}}^{\mathrm{weighted}}(\pi_{\theta}; \pi_{\mathrm{ref}}) = -\mathbb{E}_{(x, y_w, y_l) \sim \mathcal{D}} \Big[ w(x, y_w, y_l) \log \sigma \big( \beta(r_w(x) - r_l(x)) \big) \Big]. \tag{1}$$
